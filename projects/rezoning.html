STUDY 03 / QUALITATIVE POLICY RESEARCH

Whose voices shape school rezoning?

School boundaries distribute educational access—but the process of changing them is also a question of power, participation, and whose concerns institutions recognize.

The central question

How do parents influence local school rezoning processes in Richmond, Virginia? My senior capstone asks how families become involved, what resources they use to organize, and how school officials respond to their demands during boundary changes.

School assignment is based on residential geography, which has itself been shaped by long-standing housing inequalities. Understanding rezoning therefore requires examining not only who attends public meetings, but whose interests and networks carry influence through institutional decision-making.

Why Richmond’s 2019 rezoning?

I am studying Richmond Public Schools’ completed 2019 rezoning process. The public record contains committee discussions, competing boundary proposals, community comments, and final school board decisions. The case offers a way to compare how different proposals developed and how public concerns entered the record.

One planned comparison looks at a proposed Northside elementary-school pairing that was rejected alongside a completed boundary revision. Comparing the cases does not mean assuming that parent participation caused a particular vote.

Methods & operationalization

The design combines qualitative document analysis with descriptive geographic and school-level context. I plan to inventory public comments, meeting minutes or recordings, petitions, advisory materials, proposals, and votes, then code sources consistently by date, school area, position, and form of participation.

Proposed workflow · design in progress, not findings

01Document

Trace public meetings, comments, petitions, proposals, and board deliberations.

02Code

Identify forms of involvement, network use, claims, and institutional responses.

03Contextualize

Connect participation patterns with historical school and neighborhood context without assuming individual motives.

Subject to institutional ethics approval and recruitment, I also propose a small number of semi-structured interviews to help interpret the archived record.

How I use social capital

Drawing on Pierre Bourdieu and sociological work on family engagement, I examine social capital as resources made accessible through networks and relationships. In practice, this could look like organized petitioning, access to officials, coordination with parent associations, or circulating detailed policy information.

Visible participation, however, is not the same thing as influence. I distinguish between participation in the record, resources mobilized, and signs that an argument was considered in subsequent institutional action.

Why this research matters

Public participation is meaningful only when we ask who can access the process—and how institutions respond to different communities.

Research status
